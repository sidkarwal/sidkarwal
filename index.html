Four days out, Canvas went down and took the entire pre-learning course with it

Canvas is the platform half of North American higher education is enrolled on. I rebuilt all six modules as a static site in a day, wrote a no-op SCORM shim so the courseware would run with nothing behind it, registered a domain and shipped it. The students never knew.

Five rooms ran in parallel, one industry each, every team working the same scenario: by 2050 autonomous electric vehicles dominate Indian roads and the businesses built around human driving are running out of road.

Most deal sourcing finds the companies that are easy to find. This finds the ones nobody has listed.

9,000+companies sourced and scored
592public data sources
16countries covered
2,000xcost spread across the funnel
5stages before a shortlist
3model tiers, 14 task types
I own it end to end. The architecture through to the React front end. The connector interface, the entity resolver, the tiered funnel, the scoring engine, the model gateway. The data model and the provenance layer underneath the numbers, and the call to throw the first version away and start over.

A family office wanted what a deal sourcing team finds, without hiring one

Most sourcing software finds the companies that are already easy to find, the ones already indexed, ranked and pitched to everyone else looking. This is built to find the rest: profitable, quietly run businesses that never show up on a shortlist because nobody indexed them in the first place. What falls out of a normal run looks like this, one registry ID per row, a confidence badge, a revenue band, and how many sources stand behind each number.

Version one was an agentic browser that searched the way an analyst would, and it died at a few hundred companies

It worked, technically. But two things break at once when an agent tries to reason across a whole market: it cannot hold the market in its head, and the model bill grows faster than the shortlist does. Everyone building agents hits this wall eventually. Most people respond by buying a bigger model.

So the rebuild treats model calls as the scarcest resource in the system, not the cheapest

Triage is deterministic and free, no model spend at all until a company has already earned a second look. Verification, the expensive end, costs up to two dollars a company and only runs on what survives that far. That is a two thousand times spread between the cheapest step and the priciest one, and the whole funnel exists to keep the expensive end small. The worker tracks its own spend against a daily budget and stops itself rather than running away.

Underneath it: a connector interface, a write-time entity resolver, a scoring engine and a model gateway

Architecture through to the React front end, and a decision partway through to throw the first version away rather than patch it. But the moat isn’t any of that, it is the data and the provenance layer underneath: matching on registry ID with checksum validation first, then apex domain plus country, then probabilistic matching inside a blocking key. Never on name alone, and never across a border on a name at all, because a wrongly merged company is invisible in a way a missed one is not.

Confidence and provenance sit on every field, not every record

A company can carry a verified registry number and an estimated revenue band at the same time, and the interface says which is which, down to what sourced each individual field. Every model output has to pass a cite-or-unknown check, then retries, then escalates to a stronger model, then goes to a human. Nothing low-confidence gets accepted silently, and when the daily budget runs out the worker pauses and picks up again the next day rather than overspending quietly.

The sourcing and trust layers are built. The reasoning layer isn’t, yet

Everything above ships. What follows doesn’t: a system that notices the moment a company becomes sellable instead of waiting for the next scan, and writes the memo a partner would actually read, held to the same rule as everything else here, cite what’s verified, flag what isn’t, never invent the rest.

What was mine. The name. The brand and the design system. The event design. The delegate communications and the print. The interviews, from the setup to the thirty minutes with each counsellor to the post-production. The photography. Then the intelligence briefing that went to UBC leadership.

The first sprint to engage the top schools in India, built so that nobody in the room was sold to

A school counsellor is the single most influential voice in where a student applies, which is exactly why every university on earth treats her as a distribution channel. Twenty-seven came to Goa from twenty-five international schools, across Tier 1, 2 and 3 cities. Between them they advise around three and a half thousand senior students a year.

Three UBC professors spent months building sessions for counsellors, not adapting lectures they already had

Three sessions built from nothing for an audience they had never taught: the hidden curriculum, teaching in the age of AI, and the foundations of academic success. Each one shaped around what a counsellor actually does with a student, rather than around what a university wants said about itself. They cared whether it was genuinely useful to these people, and months of work went in on that basis alone. It is the reason the room worked.

The whole thing was designed on one premise, that a counsellor is the most important stakeholder a university has

Three residential days, one room, faculty present throughout and no students anywhere in it. A counsellor put the value of that better than any brief could: a chance to meet faculty one on one and talk about the realities of admissions, chances and limitations. Not a session about how to apply. A conversation between professionals.

Everything they touched over the three days was made for them

The name, the brand and the design system. The delegate communications. The certificates and the name tags. Ninety-four per cent were very satisfied with the venue and hospitality, which is the number that tells you whether people felt looked after rather than processed.

Every counsellor also sat for a thirty minute recorded interview, which is where the real return was

Not testimonials. A research instrument built into the event, which turned three days into country-specific intelligence for UBC and an eighteen point briefing for its leadership. Fourteen counsellors, interviewed separately, independently described the same gap: a seventeen year old is looked after in India and treated as an adult from the first day of a Canadian degree. Nobody had asked them before.

Goa was version one, and it was built from the first day to be run again

Every respondent rated it excellent and every one said they would return, on seventeen responses. The next versions are going to China and Indonesia. The ambition is not another summit: it is to build this into the premier school counsellor event in the world, carrying UBC’s care into every market it wants to be understood in.

The go to market, the brand and the website. The buyer persona and the regulatory groundwork underneath all of it. The positioning and the strategy. Ten training programme pages. The LinkedIn presence. The search layer, and the comparison guide for buyers weighing one programme against another.

Before anything could be written, somebody had to write down who actually buys cybersecurity training

A Singapore headquartered company selling across the region, with real programmes and a buyer nobody had described. So the first month went into research rather than output: who this person is, what they answer for, and what makes them pick up the phone.

A Singapore security chief answers to the chief executive far more often than an American one does

Thirty-five per cent report to the CEO, against three per cent in the United States. That single difference changes the entire pitch. You are not persuading a technical buyer to escalate something upward; you are talking to somebody already personally answerable to the board. Eighty per cent of them are burnt out and fifty-seven per cent never switch off, which tells you what tone to take and what not to waste their time with.

And the regulator, not the buyer, sets the clock: one hour to report an incident, not seventy-two

MAS gives financial institutions sixty minutes to notify. Multi-factor authentication is mandatory on every administrative account. There is a separate framework from the privacy regulator and another for critical information infrastructure. None of that is background colour. It is the pressure the buyer lives inside, and positioning written without understanding it would have been guesswork.

The country is short four thousand security professionals, which happens to be exactly what the company sells

The top ranked pain in the research turned out to be the product. Four hundred to eight hundred roles open every month and nobody to fill them, against a company whose entire business is training people to do the work. The positioning was not invented, it was found, and that is the argument for spending the first month reading instead of writing.

Everything a buyer meets was then built on that one person

The brand. Ten training programme pages, from security awareness for staff and for the C-suite through penetration testing, red teaming, SOC analysis, threat intelligence, cloud security, secure development and AI defence. The LinkedIn presence, the search layer, a comparison guide for buyers weighing one programme against another, and the site itself.

A permanent home for the founder-led software companies of Asia-Pacific. I built the brand from scratch, and the site, on my own.

The brand from scratch, and the website. The positioning and the language. The identity. The build. The social presence. Built for the client through 2025.

JumpTech buys profitable software companies from the people who founded them, and then never sells

A forever-hold philosophy, applied to niche enterprise software across Asia-Pacific: cybersecurity, education, logistics, healthcare. They are not a fund looking for an exit in five years, and almost everything about how a company like that has to present itself follows from that one fact.

Which means the brand is not selling an acquisition. It is asking somebody to hand over the thing they spent a decade building.

That is a harder brief than it sounds. The founder on the other side is not comparing offers on price alone. They are trying to work out whether the company will still be recognisable in three years, whether their team will be dismantled, and whether the person they are about to sell to will still be there.

So the promise had to read as continuation rather than exit

Founders stay in control, with equity and operational autonomy. Teams are kept independent and integration happens only where it genuinely helps. Fair valuations, flexible structures, and a safe home for a founder’s legacy. Every one of those phrases is aimed at a specific fear, and none of them lead with returns.

And one line had to separate them from every private equity firm a founder had already met

They scale founder-led software through strategy, not just capital. It sets the whole position: the money is the least interesting thing on offer, and what actually comes with it is regional reach, shared systems and operating discipline. The rest of the site is that sentence, proved.

The brand had to work on two audiences who want opposite reassurances

A founder needs to hear that nothing will change. The people funding the buying need to hear that a great deal will change, and quickly. Holding both without either one sounding hollow is the actual design problem, and it is why the operating code, acquisition through integration to growth and scale, is stated as plainly as it is.
